# Twelfth benchmark: 100 simulation runs at once on the 10,000-element view (M12)

Date: 2026-10-31. Measured once, by a builder, on the branch that builds M12's benchmark (`?simulate`, ADR 0085). By the user's decision of 2026-10-27, benchmark marks do not hold a milestone back: what missed is recorded here, and nothing was optimized.

## The machine

- **Not the reference laptop** of `benchmark-3.md`: an Intel Core i9-14900HX (32 logical processors), 32 GB of memory, an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Laptop GPU, Windows 11.
- **The browser:** Chrome 156.0.8078.74 run headless (`--headless=new`) on that GPU through ANGLE and Direct3D 11, in a profile of its own on a spare debugging port, with `--disable-background-timer-throttling`, `--disable-renderer-backgrounding` and `--disable-backgrounding-occluded-windows`, a window of 1600 by 900 pixels (1578 by 802 inside) at a pixel ratio of 1, cross-origin isolated. No browser window was visible.
- **The build:** `pnpm bench`'s, built with its config and served on 127.0.0.1:47707, which was free.
- **Quiet:** no test run, benchmark or other builder ran meanwhile; the OneDrive sync service, which the repository lives under, kept about one of the 32 processors busy throughout.
- **Headless frames came faster than 60 a second:** with the canvas at rest, frames as shown came every 4.2 to 5.7 ms, so frames mode here times frames as fast as the browser offers them, not paced at 60 Hz.

## How it was measured

- **The project** (`bench/simulateProject.ts`): the synthetic view of 10,000 elements, of which 100 contents of systems, spread evenly over the view, are created from "Simulated service", a component of the benchmark's own package. It simulates `load`, a number between 0 and 1,000 that wanders by chance each tick, and `trend`, one of three words; it plays up to 10,000 ticks of 1 s. `load` is also an attribute of the component, and the view's overlay colors every shape's fill by `load`, so that while a run is shown the overlay reads its simulated value through the attribute lookup's `simulated` map. Each tick marks the element and one of its relations drawn as lines, another one each tick where it has several: 100 highlighted shapes, 100 highlighted lines and 100 markers every tick.
- **The code:** the package's code is generated by the benchmark, and approved as the person would approve it: handed to the code worker with its hash (`codeHash` of its text and capabilities, none) and nothing granted. The real code worker ran it in QuickJS: the page could start it, and no fallback was needed. Where it cannot, the page measures with the worker's `SimulationDriver` in the page instead, and says so.
- **100 runs need 7 code workers.** A tab runs at most 16 runs at once (`SIMULATION_LIMITS.runsAtOnce`, ADR 0085), and each worker's driver refuses a 17th. The 100 runs were shared among 7 code workers, 14 or 15 each, each its own `CodeHost` given the project and the package; one `SimulationStore` held every run, as the tab's would. This is more than the app can do today (see "Questions"). One worker with 16 runs, as many as the app plays, was measured after them.
- **The canvas's side, as the shell does it, without React:** each frame, `store.frame(now)`; the frames `SimulationMarks` asked for; then, whenever `store.simulated()` changed, the lookup of that moment, the canvas's scene with its values (`liveSceneOf`), its index, and `controller.update`. A tick's marks went through `SimulationMarks` into an emphasis store, then to `controller.setEmphasis`.
- **The phases:**
  - pan at the whole view with the 100 runs open and paused, as a baseline;
  - at 60 ticks a second, then as fast as it goes: the camera still at the whole view (300 frames), panning at the whole view (180) and panning where names show (180). Each run starts again from tick 0 before the speed's first phase, and before each phase as fast as it goes, so that no run reaches its 10,000 ticks while measured;
  - 16 runs in one code worker at each speed, the camera still (300 frames).

  Work mode times each frame's work, GPU included; frames mode times frames as shown. Both judge by the 10,000-element marks: 10 ms on average, 13 ms at the 95th percentile and 33.3 ms at worst in work mode; 18.1 ms, 25 ms and 33.3 ms in frames mode.
- **A tick's time to the canvas:** from the store receiving it, or from the start of the frame before the one that showed it when it came earlier, since a tick held ahead cannot show before it falls due, to the end of the frame that drew it. In work mode that end is the frame's start plus its measured time, GPU included; in frames mode it is the canvas's next frame drawn (`afterNextFrame`). The mark is two frames, 33.3 ms. Ticks skipped as fast as it goes, which the store never shows, are not counted.
- **Ticks a second:** for each run, the ticks it went on the canvas over the time of the phases it played in, then the median, the 95th percentile, the slowest and the fastest of the runs.
- **Two diagnostic runs, beside the official ones:** `&marks=none`, where the ticks' marks are not drawn, and `&marks=still`, where the markers stand still at their ends, as under reduced motion. They tell the marks' cost apart from the rest.

The workers run on other processors, so this is likely the laptop sharing its power among busy cores, not the page holding the workers back.

## What it says

- **The overlay reading simulated values is cheap.** With 100 runs changing their values every frame, the scene with its values, its index and the canvas's update take a few milliseconds a frame. Without the marks the canvas keeps its 60 frames per second and a tick shows within two frames, at 60 and as fast as it goes.
- **The canvas misses its 60 frames per second target because of a tick's marks.** Highlighting 100 shapes and 100 lines and sending 100 markers each tick costs about 45 to 50 ms a frame at the whole view, and travelling markers more than double it at 60 ticks a second, about 120 ms. Where names show, the marks cost less (25 to 45 ms), and the frames come faster with them.
- **A tick does not show within two frames** while the marks are drawn: about two of the canvas's frames, 92 to 232 ms at the median. Without them it shows in 7 to 13 ms at the median.
- **Ticks a second:** every run reached its 60 at 60 ticks a second, 100 at once in seven workers as 16 in one. As fast as it goes, each of 100 runs reached about 265 ticks a second at the median and 235 at the slowest, or 445 at the median when the page drew no marks.
- **The paused baseline** misses by its worst frames and, in work mode, by its 95th percentile (19 ms): the 100 highlights and markers of tick 0 are drawn at the whole view while the camera pans. Without marks its worst frame was 62 ms in work mode and 21 ms in frames mode, from the first frames of the pan.

## What it points at, for whoever takes it up

Nothing here is in the benchmark's own code, which does what the shell does. The changes are in the canvas's renderer and in `simulation/marks.ts`, and were not made:

1. **The renderer draws highlights and markers as world-space strokes** (`drawLit`, `drawMarkers` and `traceTrail` in `canvas/renderer.ts`), with widths of a few pixels divided by the scale and round joins and caps. PixiJS works out a round join's or cap's segments from its radius in local units (`15 × angle × √radius / π`), so at the whole view, at a scale of 0.052, a 10-pixel halo is 194 units wide and each cap takes about 150 segments. Every change of emphasis rebuilds the whole layer: 100 rings, corners, halos and lines, with 8 pieces of trail for each marker. A tick at a time, that is tens of thousands of segments a frame. Drawing these marks in screen space, so that their radii are pixels, or with joins and caps of a fixed segment count far out, would be where to start. Keeping each highlight as its own small object and moving markers as sprites would also do it, so that a tick changes which are shown rather than rebuilding all of them.
2. **The highlights are rebuilt when nothing they show moved.** Every tick changes the 100 simulating shapes' fill, so the scene's shapes are new objects. The renderer's emphasis keeps its highlights only while the shapes are the same objects (`keptEmphasized`), so it draws them again for the new scene as well as for the new marks. Comparing highlights by key would leave the halos alone when only a fill changed.
3. **`SimulationMarks` sets the emphasis twice a frame at a finite speed:** once when the store's tick changes, and again from its own frame while markers travel. Each time the renderer draws the markers again. Updating once a frame, from its own frame callback scheduled when the store changes, would halve that.

## Questions for the main session

- **100 runs at once is more than the app plays.** ADR 0085 and `SIMULATION_LIMITS.runsAtOnce` let a tab play at most 16 runs, and the worker's driver refuses a 17th, while M12's "Done when" asks the benchmark for 100 elements simulating at once. The benchmark shares the 100 runs among 7 code workers, each within the limit, which the app never does. Either the "Done when" means 16, or the limit should rise, which the numbers above allow on the worker's side: 16 runs in one worker go as fast as 100 in seven, and at 60 ticks a second each worker had time to spare.
